# Decathlon.in — Heuristic Evaluation of the Purchase Journey

<Meta>Heuristic evaluation · Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics · Single evaluator · Self-initiated, unaffiliated · September 2026</Meta>

I bought a cycle on Decathlon.in. It cannot be returned — and nothing on the site tells the shopper that before they pay.

This is an eight-finding heuristic evaluation of the full purchase journey (search → payment), mapped to Jakob Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics. Three findings are high priority. All three are fixable without a redesign.

## Task and method

**Task.** Buy a cycle for exercise, start to finish: home page to payment confirmation, the way an ordinary shopper would.

**Method.** Walked step by step against seven guiding questions on discoverability, pricing, cart control, policy clarity and checkout feedback; each friction point mapped to the heuristic it violates. Six heuristics were the original focus; a seventh — Consistency and Standards — surfaced on its own during testing.

1. Can I compare products without opening many tabs?
2. Are delivery charges shown early enough?
3. Are taxes and fees transparent?
4. Can quantity be changed or an item removed easily?
5. Are return and warranty conditions understandable?
6. Is "Buy now" / "Add to cart" visually clear?
7. Does checkout show progress?

**Funnel.** Home → Search → Product page → Cart → Delivery → Payment.

## Highest-impact finding

### This cycle cannot actually be returned

Buried in the return policy's eligibility table — reachable only via a footer link, several scrolls down the product page — is the line that mattered most before checkout: Bulky/Technical Equipment, the category covering cycles, is not eligible for return or refund. Only a replacement within 30 days is available; even a size or colour exchange is off the table.

Nothing on the product page, cart or checkout discloses this. A shopper who assumes the standard 30-day return shown elsewhere on the same page learns otherwise only by going looking for it — usually after paying.

**Business impact.** This is the kind of gap that turns into a return dispute, a support ticket or a public complaint — discovered after the sale, not before it.

**Heuristics implicated.** Recognition rather than recall · Match between system and the real world.

## Findings by heuristic

### Visibility of system status

Clicking "Proceed to pay" triggers a 15–20 second wait with no spinner, progress bar or message — long enough that I reconsidered the ₹6,999 purchase mid-wait. A step progress bar does exist, but its fill colour barely contrasts with the white background. *Recommendation:* add a loading indicator with reassuring microcopy for the payment redirect; restyle the progress bar with real contrast.

### Match between system and the real world

The homepage opens with a full-width discount banner before any products, and category pages repeat the pattern. Real-world shopping runs the other way: item, then price, then discount. The delivery charge is labelled "Convenience fee" and is not shown until the delivery step, three clicks into checkout. *Recommendation:* lead templates with products; rename to "Delivery fee" and surface it on the cart page.

### Consistency and standards

There is no way to compare two products side by side — comparing the BTWIN against the Rockrider meant a second browser tab. "Add to cart" permanently swaps to "Go to cart"; there is no way to add a second unit without refreshing, which then loses that state again. *Recommendation:* a lightweight compare tray (2–3 items); replace the one-way button swap with a persistent quantity stepper, as the cart page already does.

### User control and freedom

Checking delivery availability and cost requires being logged in with a saved address — no pincode-only preview, unlike every other browsing step. And there is no "Buy now": every purchase is routed through Add to cart → Cart → Delivery → Payment. *Recommendation:* pincode-only delivery check before login; consider a "Buy now" path for single-item purchases.

### Error prevention and recovery

The same "−" control behaves two ways: with two units it decrements; at exactly one unit it does nothing — no message, no disabled state. The only way to remove the item is a separate trash icon, found after several dead clicks. A control that looks live but does nothing is exactly where shoppers abandon carts or file "broken site" complaints. *Recommendation:* make the state visible — grey it out or swap to the trash icon at quantity one — and say so when an action cannot complete.

### Recognition rather than recall

Return and warranty terms are not shown on the product page or the cart — the two places a shopper is actually deciding. The only path is a footer link, and the footer does not exist on the cart page at all. The section that answers "can I return this?" sits below several paragraphs of repeated boilerplate. *Recommendation:* a short return/warranty summary on the product page; a consistent footer; the eligibility table above the intro text.

## What would move the needle

### Ship in two weeks — no roadmap needed

- Rename "Convenience fee" to "Delivery fee"
- Restyle the checkout progress bar for real contrast
- Add a one-line return/refund disclosure on cycle and bulky-item pages
- Add a loading spinner for the payment redirect

### Needs a product decision

- Product comparison across listings
- Persistent "add to cart" as a quantity stepper, not a one-way button
- Pincode-only delivery check before requiring login
- Fix the qty "−" control's silent failure at Qty = 1
- Reordered templates: products before promos

Six of the ten heuristics surfaced friction somewhere in a single purchase — most of it fixable without a redesign, just a more honest default: show the cost, the policy and the state of the system before the shopper has to go looking for them.

## Limitations

Single evaluator, single task, one session on desktop. Findings are expert-review hypotheses, not measured usability rates or prevalence estimates. No analytics or support-ticket data was available.
